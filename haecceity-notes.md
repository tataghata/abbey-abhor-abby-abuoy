# HAECCEITY

*Thisness: the difficulty of being this one.*

**Approximate pronunciation:** heck-SEE-uh-tee. English pronunciations vary by accent and dictionary.

**Working definition:** The principle by which a thing is this individual: not merely an example of a kind, and not merely a unique collection of describable traits.

## The question beneath the word

Imagine learning everything normally offered in a description of a stone: its mineral composition, shape, mass, temperature, history, location, and the irregularities visible under magnification. These descriptions may allow you to identify it unerringly. But identification is one achievement; explaining what makes an individual individual is another. Why is this stone one particular being rather than the common nature “stone” appearing without any determinate individuality? What makes it *this one*?

“Haecceity” names a proposed answer to that metaphysical question. Its strangeness comes from trying to articulate something ordinary language already presupposes whenever we say *this*. We encounter individuals constantly; explaining individuality proves harder than recognizing an individual across a room. The word does not mean an especially vivid personality, an ineffable atmosphere, or an object's most distinctive feature. Those associations can enrich poetry, but they should not silently replace the philosophical problem.

## A demonstrative becomes a noun

The English word comes from scholastic Latin *haecceitas*, conventionally translated as “thisness.” Its linguistic ancestry runs through *haec*, a form of the Latin demonstrative *hic, haec, hoc*—“this”—and the emphatic form *haecce*. The abstract-noun ending *-itas* turns the demonstrative family into a name for a principle or condition. English “thisness” performs a similar conversion: a word that points becomes a word about what makes pointing toward a particular possible.

The grammatical history does not itself establish the metaphysics. Latin gender does not imply that individuality is feminine, and attaching a noun ending to “this” does not demonstrate that there must be a separate entity called thisness. The linguistic ingenuity is a starting point: it lets philosophy hold still a question normally carried, almost unnoticed, by our demonstratives.

## Scotus: a nature and its individual

The doctrine is especially associated with the medieval philosopher and theologian John Duns Scotus. His problem of individuation concerns how a common nature becomes the nature of this singular thing. Humanity, considered as a nature, tells us what humans are. It does not, just by doing so, tell us why Socrates is this human and another human is a different individual.

Scotus's account is subtler than a picture of a universal substance being sliced into people. A nature considered in itself is not already universal in the way a concept is universal; neither does its intelligible content, by itself, supply the numerical individuality of a particular being. Scotus attributes to a common nature a unity less than numerical unity. This allows him to say that nature is not a mere arbitrary fiction without identifying the natures of different individuals as one numerically identical object.

The individual difference completes the explanation of this individual's singularity. In the vocabulary associated with the doctrine, its haecceity is a positive, intrinsic principle of individuation. “Positive” matters: individuality is not explained merely by saying the thing fails to be another thing. “Intrinsic” matters too: its fundamental individuality is not simply bestowed by our labels, our attention, or an accidental address.

Nor should haecceity be imagined as a microscopic identity badge hidden inside matter. Scotus's distinction between a nature and its individual difference belongs to his technical metaphysical account; it does not require two independently detachable objects. His discussions of formal distinction help resist both the crude additive picture and the suggestion that the distinction is only a verbal convenience.

## What it is, and which one it is

*Quiddity* is another scholastic word, derived from Latin *quid*, “what.” Roughly, it concerns what a thing is: its nature or essence. Haecceity concerns its being this particular individual. The distinction is not between an ordinary description and a sufficiently elaborate description. Adding qualifications can make a description more selective without settling the metaphysical basis of singularity.

A barcode may pick out exactly one manufactured item. The item does not acquire its original individuality when the barcode is attached. A uniquely identifying biography can enable recognition; it need not explain why the subject was already an individual before the biography existed. Likewise, “the only cup on this table” may succeed as a description today and fail tomorrow, although the cup persists. The conditions of our successful reference can change without individuality appearing and disappearing with them.

## Two objects, one careful illustration

The accompanying image uses two visually matching objects to place **qualitative similarity** beside **numerical distinctness**. They share an apparent form, yet the composition presents two objects. “Qualitative” concerns what they are like; “numerical” concerns whether we are counting one thing or more than one. Two things can resemble each other very closely while remaining two.

There is an essential caveat: the illustrated objects occupy different locations. They therefore differ in spatial relations, and a picture cannot establish that they share every qualitative property. Even perfect agreement in visible shape and color would not prove agreement in composition, history, or dispositions. The illustration makes a distinction accessible; it does not demonstrate the possibility of two objects indiscernible in every respect.

More demanding thought experiments ask whether an entirely symmetrical world could contain two qualitatively indiscernible objects. Such cases lead into debates about the identity of indiscernibles, relational discernibility, and what counts as a qualitative property. Haecceity offers one family of answers to individuation, but the simple sight of a matching pair does not settle those debates in its favor.

## Knowing this one is not making it this one

An epistemic question asks how we know, recognize, or distinguish an individual. A metaphysical question asks what the individual's being an individual consists in. Fingerprints, memory, serial numbers, and trajectories can help with the first question. Whether they provide the final answer to the second depends on an account of objects and identity that must itself be defended.

This distinction also limits what introspection can establish. Feeling that someone has an irreducible presence may be psychologically powerful, but the feeling is not direct proof of a Scotistic individual difference. Conversely, our inability to distinguish two objects does not establish that there is only one. Ignorance and individuality are different kinds of fact.

## Can “this” say what a description cannot?

A demonstrative such as “this” can refer without listing a complete set of properties. Context, attention, and often a gesture help determine its reference. That makes demonstrative language an inviting companion to haecceity: both seem to approach a particular without first assembling a descriptive inventory.

But an act of pointing and a principle of individuation must not be conflated. A speaker can point ambiguously; a photograph can be mislabeled; “this” can fail to secure a reference. Those failures concern language and its use. The successful use of a demonstrative also does not, by itself, prove that its referent contains an irreducible metaphysical thisness. A theory of reference and a theory of individuation can inform each other while remaining different projects.

## The tempting mistakes

Calling haecceity “the unique bundle of traits that makes you you” supplies an appealing slogan but changes the doctrine. Bundle theories attempt, in different ways, to account for objects through their properties or constituents. Scotistic individuation does not simply mean that enough ordinary qualities have accumulated to make a unique profile. Even a unique combination leaves open why those qualities belong to one concrete individual and what grounds its singularity.

“Personal brand” is farther away still. A brand selects and communicates recognizable features for an audience. Haecceity does not increase with memorability, originality, fame, or expressive style. An anonymous, thoroughly unremarkable thing poses the problem of individuation just as fully as a celebrated person. Thisness is not a prize awarded to what catches our attention.

Modern philosophers also use “haecceity” in discussions of possible worlds and identity across possibilities. There, a haecceity may be construed as an individual-specific property, such as being identical with a particular individual. These later uses raise related questions, but they should not be treated as straightforward reproductions of Scotus's medieval framework.

## What an objection can still ask

A critic can ask whether haecceity explains individuality or merely gives it a technical name. If the individual difference cannot be specified through general qualities, how does invoking it illuminate the individual? A defender can reply that an explanation need not reduce every feature of reality to repeatable descriptions: individuality may require a primitive or irreducible principle. The disagreement then concerns what kinds of explanation metaphysics should accept.

A second challenge concerns alternatives. Perhaps matter, spatiotemporal continuity, causal history, relations, or some combination supplies everything needed for individuation. Evaluating that suggestion requires attending to the kinds of entities under discussion and to what the proposed account must explain. A criterion useful for tracking material objects does not automatically settle every question about persons, events, or immaterial beings.

A third challenge concerns intelligibility. If we can understand natures in common terms, how can we understand an individuating principle without turning it into another common term? This is a pressure within the project, not a reason to replace careful argument with reverent vagueness. “Beyond description” is not a license to say anything whatever.

## One deliberately difficult sentence

> Beneath the twice-told likeness of the counterfeiting dawn, the unrepeatable this—neither jewel of adjective nor ghost behind the noun—keeps its unborrowable appointment with existence, while every what, however minutely named, arrives wearing the livery of possible company, and the pointing finger, having exhausted the world's descriptive weather, discovers that to indicate a singular is not yet to explain its singularity.

This original sentence uses “twice-told likeness” for resemblance and “jewel of adjective” for a particularly impressive descriptive trait. The “ghost” rejects the picture of a hidden duplicate object. “Possible company” suggests the repeatability of common descriptions. The exhausted pointing finger marks the central restraint: successfully indicating an individual does not complete an account of individuation. The sentence is an imaginative compression, not a quotation from Joyce or a substitute for argument.

Translations, selections, and numbering conventions should be checked against the edition in hand. Introductory scholarship on Scotus's metaphysics can help situate common nature, individual difference, and formal distinction before comparison with contemporary debates about identity and possible worlds.
