# Chiasmus

*ky-AZ-muhs · The second crossing changes the first*

Chiasmus is a crossed arrangement in which elements presented in one order recur in reverse order, often described as ABBA; the reversal may involve grammatical structures, concepts, or, in a narrower related figure, repeated words.

From Greek chiasmos, a crossing or arrangement crosswise, related to the letter chi, written like X. The name makes a visible figure of reversed correspondence, although actual examples may operate through syntax or meaning rather than literal intersecting lines.

## Order becomes an argument

A phrase can place persons before institutions, then institutions before persons, making the reversal itself carry a claim about responsibility. The pattern supplies balance and memorability. It does not prove the relationship it asserts, because a symmetrical sentence can arrange weak reasoning as elegantly as strong reasoning.

## A neighboring exact reversal

Antimetabole usually names the repetition of the same words in reversed order, while chiasmus can involve a broader reversal of grammatical or conceptual relations. Terminology varies among rhetorical traditions. Stating which elements cross is more useful than policing every historical example into a perfectly rigid taxonomy.

## Scale changes the evidence

Chiasmus can appear within a short phrase or be proposed as an organizing pattern across a much longer passage. Larger claims require stronger evidence about boundaries and correspondences. Selective grouping can manufacture apparent symmetry, especially when intervening material or inconvenient differences are quietly removed from the analysis.

## Return without sameness

The second occurrence of an element arrives after its counterpart has changed the context. Reversal can therefore create development rather than mere repetition: what began as an agent returns as an object, or what appeared subordinate becomes governing. The crossing can question hierarchy as easily as reinforce it.

## An original literary sentence

> She made a home of the language that had made a stranger of her, and the stranger, learning to house what had unhoused her, found every returning word crossing the threshold in another person’s shoes.

## Working through the word

Consider the invented sentence “We shape the paths that later shape us.” It repeats the verb while reversing the relation between human agents and the routes they create. The practical claim is that built environments influence future behavior. The crossed structure compresses reciprocal causation into a memorable shape. To decide whether the claim is true in a particular case, however, one still needs evidence about the environment and the people who use it. Rhetorical fit and empirical fit are different achievements.

For a deliberately simple structural example, take “I admire her courage; her patience I respect.” The first clause places subject and verb before object; the second fronts an object before its subject and verb. The correspondence is grammatical rather than a perfect repetition of identical words. A classic antimetabole instead reverses lexical items themselves. These distinctions help describe the machinery of a passage, although handbooks and scholars do not always use the labels in exactly the same way.

There is a temptation to discover vast crossed structures in any sufficiently rich work. An analyst can choose broad themes for the outer units, narrow themes for the inner units, omit awkward passages, and thereby produce an attractive diagram. A persuasive account should explain why its units belong where they do, demonstrate comparable levels of specificity, and consider alternative arrangements. The more flexible the matching rule becomes, the less surprising the resulting symmetry is. Close reading benefits from formal imagination disciplined by textual resistance.

The original dense sentence works through a series of crossings: language makes a stranger, the stranger makes a home, and the home must accommodate what once excluded its inhabitant. The sequence is an extended meditation on reversal rather than a perfectly exhaustive four-term diagram. It includes echoes that exceed a strict ABBA pattern. Naming those limits is more instructive than declaring every attractive recurrence an exact chiasmus.

For further reading, consult Richard A. Lanham’s A Handlist of Rhetorical Terms and compare close readings that explicitly identify the linguistic units they treat as corresponding. The locally rendered paired strands offer a metaphor of crossing and reciprocal transformation. Their physical symmetry does not imply that a well made rhetorical sentence is logically valid, politically fair, or historically accurate; those judgments require arguments beyond its formal grace.
