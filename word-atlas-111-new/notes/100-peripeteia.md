# 100 · peripeteia

The action turns against the direction that carried it

## Definition

A reversal in the direction of an action or situation, especially in drama; in Aristotle's account, an effective reversal arises according to probability or necessity within the plot's own causal development.

## Etymology

From Greek peripeteia, reversal or sudden change, related to peripiptein, to fall around or encounter a turn; its dramatic sense is classical.

## Reversal belongs to action

Peripeteia concerns a turn toward an opposing result in an action or situation. A messenger arrives to reassure someone, yet the message exposes catastrophe. The reversal is stronger than a change in emotional weather: the direction of what is happening is altered through the unfolding events themselves.

## Aristotle asks for connection

In Aristotle's account of effective plotting, reversal should arise according to probability or necessity within the action. The surprising turn becomes intelligible through established relations. A random accident may change someone's fortunes, but it does not automatically possess the explanatory force Aristotle values in a well-constructed plot.

## Recognition can intensify it

Anagnorisis, recognition or discovery, can coincide with reversal when newly understood facts transform a character's position. These concepts are distinct: a discovery need not reverse fortune, and a reversal need not immediately clarify its cause. Their combination can make the movement of a tragedy especially concentrated and powerful.

## Surprise must be earned

A concealed coincidence can startle a reader without illuminating the preceding action. A stronger reversal may reorganize that action retrospectively, making earlier details matter differently. Analysis asks whether the turn develops from the story's relationships or merely interrupts them with an event whose main function is to astonish.

## Further exploration

A messenger arrives intending to reassure a ruler, but the information instead exposes the ruler's disaster. That pattern illustrates peripeteia: an action's movement turns toward an opposing result. Aristotle's discussion of Oedipus provides a famous example, closely connecting reversal with recognition. The term does not simply name any surprise, bad luck, or change in mood. A contrived accident can reverse fortunes without possessing the causal force Aristotle values. Dramatic power comes when the turn astonishes at first yet becomes intelligible through the relationships the story has already established.

Imagine a character entering a competition to save a threatened institution. The victory secures attention that exposes the institution's concealed wrongdoing, making success the means of its collapse. This sketch could become an earned reversal if the earlier plot establishes the concealment, the publicity, and the character's reasons for competing. If those conditions appear only in the final paragraph, surprise may replace causality. The difference is not simply whether the ending is happy or disastrous. Peripeteia asks how the action turns.
