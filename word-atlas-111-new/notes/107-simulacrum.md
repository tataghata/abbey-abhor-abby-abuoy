# 107 · simulacrum

The model can become a measure for its supposed original

## Definition

An image, likeness, or representation; in some modern theoretical uses, especially Baudrillard's, a simulation whose relation to an original becomes unstable, displaced, or no longer sufficient to explain its social reality.

## Etymology

From Latin simulacrum, likeness, image, or representation, related to simulare, to make like; later theories transform rather than erase the ordinary meaning.

## The ordinary likeness

A simulacrum can simply be an image, likeness, or representation. That broad historical sense remains relevant even when later theory gives the word more specialized force. A statue or replica need not immediately exemplify every philosophical account associated with the term merely because it resembles another thing.

## The authority can reverse

A souvenir village might become the standard by which visitors judge the actual settlement. Residents then alter buildings to match the idealized model. Representation is no longer only following its supposed original; it helps organize expectations and behavior that transform the reality whose authenticity it appears to certify.

## Baudrillard's particular challenge

Baudrillard investigates signs and simulations whose relation to an original becomes unstable or insufficient to explain their social reality. His account is more demanding than announcing that modern things are fake. Different philosophers use simulacrum differently, so attribution and conceptual setting matter when explaining what kind of displacement is claimed.

## Artificial is not automatically false

A model can be explicitly artificial and still provide reliable knowledge. A replica can preserve access to a fragile object. The critical question concerns how representations acquire authority, circulate, and organize experience, rather than whether an object is manufactured or whether its materials happen to be authentically old.

## Further exploration

A statue can be called a simulacrum in the ordinary sense of a likeness. Baudrillard's influential account asks more unsettling questions about signs and models that organize experience rather than merely copy an independently available original. A themed environment may become the standard by which visitors judge the places it ostensibly imitates. That does not make every replica an instance of the same theoretical condition, and other philosophers use the term differently. The critical issue is the shifting authority of representation, not the easy accusation that anything artificial is automatically false.

Imagine tourists who reject a real landscape because its colors fail to match heavily edited promotional pictures. The images have become a standard against which the place is found deficient. This example provides an approachable opening into the problem, but it should not be treated as a complete demonstration of Baudrillard's theory. Ask which institutions distribute the pictures, what incentives sustain them, and how expectations feed back into the place's design. A critical account needs those relations. The sculpture reverses the expected direction of imitation by letting its central miniature export a pattern; it does not declare that the surrounding houses are unreal, only that their authority as originals has become complicated.

## Original literary sentence

The village rebuilt itself to resemble the souvenir sold at its station, until the miniature's painted shutters became the measure of authenticity and the old crooked windows apologized for being real.
