# 097 · ontogenesis

An individual arrives through organized becoming

## Definition

The coming-into-being and development of an individual organism or entity; in biology it overlaps with ontogeny, while philosophical accounts may examine individuation as a process rather than presupposing completed individuals.

## Etymology

Formed from Greek ont-, the stem associated with being, and genesis, origin or becoming; its disciplinary meaning depends on the context.

## Development changes organization

Biological ontogenesis concerns an individual organism's coming-into-being and development. An acorn becoming a sapling changes more than dimensions: tissues differentiate, structures emerge, and relationships among parts develop. The concept directs attention to the processes through which a living individual acquires and transforms its organized capacities over time.

## Different histories, different questions

Ontogeny concerns individual development; phylogeny concerns the evolutionary history of lineages. Morphogenesis focuses especially on the formation of shape. These inquiries intersect, but they are not interchangeable. Explaining how one leaf develops is different from explaining how a lineage acquired a characteristic leaf form across generations.

## Individuation as a problem

In Simondon's philosophical work, inquiry into ontogenesis emphasizes individuation: the individual emerges through processes that a description of finished individuals alone cannot adequately explain. This broader use is not merely another name for embryology. It changes the starting point of inquiry from completed entities to their coming about.

## The sequence is a simplification

A row of developmental stages makes transformation legible, but real development includes feedback, environmental relations, and overlapping processes. It is not necessarily a march toward a single predetermined visible form. A careful account asks which conditions constrain development and which possibilities remain open at a particular stage.

## Further exploration

An acorn becoming a sapling illustrates individual development, with changes in organization as well as size. Ontogenesis in this biological sense differs from phylogenesis, the evolutionary history of lineages, and from morphogenesis considered specifically as the formation of shape. In Gilbert Simondon's philosophy, the focus widens to individuation: individuals emerge through processes that cannot be fully explained by starting with already finished individuals. This philosophical use should not be casually equated with embryology. Across contexts, the word directs attention toward how a being becomes, not merely toward what it presently is.

Follow a houseplant over several weeks and record changes in branching, leaf position, and response to light. A photograph at each stage captures shape, but the process also includes relations invisible in the image: water transport, metabolic activity, and environmental exchange. The sculpture compresses such development into a readable sequence while leaving that complexity implicit. In philosophy, a parallel methodological question arises: what is lost when explanation starts with already individuated things and treats their relations as secondary? Simondon's answer cannot simply be read off the plant, yet the example helps expose the problem. Becoming is not merely a finished object plus elapsed time; the organization making that object possible also requires explanation.

## Original literary sentence
