# 066 · exaptation

A useful inheritance acquires another use.

## Definition

The cooption of a biological feature for a current role different from the role for which it originally evolved, or the functional recruitment of a feature that previously had no selected function.

## Etymology

Coined by Stephen Jay Gould and Elisabeth Vrba in 1982, using ex- and adaptation to distinguish coopted usefulness from original adaptive origin.

## Meaning In Motion

Feathers that existed before powered flight illustrate how a structure can later contribute to a different function, although the evolutionary histories and original roles of feathers require careful evidence. Exaptation distinguishes current usefulness from historical origin: a trait's present benefit does not by itself reveal why it arose.

## The Crucial Distinction

It may subsequently undergo adaptations that improve its newer role. The term also appears metaphorically in discussions of technology, but biological exaptation involves evolutionary histories rather than an inventor's deliberate redesign. Neither the concept nor a plausible story substitutes for testing a specific historical explanation.

## Present Benefit, Past Origin

To explain why a trait is useful now is not yet to explain why it arose. A historical account needs evidence that separates earlier conditions from later recruitment. Comparing related organisms and preserved structures can help test that account, whereas a convenient story about present usefulness cannot establish it.

## Functions Can Accumulate

Cooption need not abolish an earlier role. A structure can contribute to several functions, and later selection can refine a newly acquired one. The important distinction concerns historical relations among origin, recruitment, and subsequent modification; those relations may be complex rather than a neat switch between two exclusive purposes.

## Extended reading

Imagine an architectural alcove built for storage and later used to improve acoustics. The example makes changed use intuitive, but deliberate human repurposing is only a metaphor for biological exaptation. Evolutionary histories do not require an inventor who foresees a new role. Variants, inherited structures, environmental conditions, and selection operate without that kind of planning.

The feather example likewise demands careful wording. Feathers preceded powered flight, but reconstructing their earlier roles involves evidence and continuing inquiry. A familiar classroom illustration should not harden into a claim that every relevant lineage followed one perfectly known functional sequence. Different traits can have multiple effects, and subsequent adaptations may alter structures after recruitment.

For practice, distinguish three claims about a feature: what it currently does, when that role became important, and how the feature originally arose. List the evidence each claim would require. If the same present observation is being used to settle all three, the historical inference needs further support.
