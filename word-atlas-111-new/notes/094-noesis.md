# 094 · noesis

Attend to the way consciousness is directed

## Definition

An act of thinking or intellection; in Husserlian phenomenology, the intentional act-aspect of consciousness, such as perceiving, remembering, or judging, considered in relation to what is experienced as its correlate.

## Etymology

From Greek noēsis, thinking or understanding, related to noein, to apprehend intellectually, and nous, intellect; later philosophers develop different technical uses.

## Consciousness has an aboutness

In Husserlian phenomenology, noesis concerns the intentional act-aspect of experience. Perceiving a tree, remembering it, and judging whether it is diseased are different ways of being directed toward something. “Intentional” here concerns aboutness, not merely a conscious plan to undertake an action for a practical purpose.

## The correlate belongs beside it

Noesis is analyzed in relation to noema, the correlate of what is experienced as meant. The distinction helps describe how an object is intended through an act. It does not identify two physical components housed in the brain, nor does it by itself explain experience through a neurological mechanism.

## Reflecting changes the question

Ordinary attention asks whether the tree is tall or healthy. Phenomenological attention can instead examine how it is perceived, remembered, expected, or doubted. The shift does not require denying the tree's existence. It changes which features of the encounter the inquiry takes as its immediate subject matter.

## A term with older uses

In other philosophical traditions, noesis can name thinking or intellectual apprehension with different technical implications. A Platonic discussion cannot simply inherit every distinction in Husserl. The word becomes precise through its setting, so a responsible explanation identifies the philosophical framework before treating its specialized claims as generally applicable.

## Further exploration

Seeing a tree, remembering that tree, and doubting whether it still stands involve different ways of being conscious of something. In Husserl's framework, attending to those act-characteristics brings the noetic side into view, while the noematic side concerns the object as intended or meant. This distinction is analytical, not a division into two physical components of the brain. In Plato and other traditions, noēsis can instead refer to intellectual apprehension. The term therefore requires an identified philosophical setting and should not be treated as a universal synonym for consciousness.

Close your eyes after looking at an ordinary object. Compare remembering its color with judging that the remembered color is accurate, then with imagining the object in an impossible color. The referent may remain stable while the character of each act changes. This exercise supplies an approachable distinction, not a claim that introspection transparently reveals every structure of consciousness. Husserl's analyses develop a demanding philosophical method, and brief demonstrations inevitably simplify it. The image likewise uses three trajectories to separate modes of approach; it does not suggest that an experience literally travels through a tube toward its object. Its task is to make a relational difference easier to hold in attention.

## Original literary sentence

She saw the tree, recalled the tree, doubted the tree, and through those changing verbs the same green question leaned toward her, each act giving its presence a different weather.
