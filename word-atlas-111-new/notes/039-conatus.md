# 039 · conatus

To remain is already to do.

## Definition

A striving or endeavor; in Spinoza's philosophy, the endeavor by which each thing, as far as its own power permits, persists in its being, expressing that thing's actual essence.

## Etymology

Latin conatus means an effort, undertaking, or attempt; it comes from conari, to attempt or endeavor, rather than from a word for consciousness.

## Meaning In Motion

Conatus is broader than a conscious wish to survive. For Spinoza, a body and a mind express their power of existing through characteristic activity, even when no deliberate intention is involved. A plant's growth offers an accessible illustration, although the concept belongs to a general metaphysics rather than to a botanical explanation.

## The Crucial Distinction

Human desire is related to this endeavor under awareness. Crucially, conatus does not guarantee successful preservation: external causes can overwhelm a thing, and understanding one's striving is different from morally endorsing every impulse it produces. Its limits remain causal, not merely motivational.

## Essence In Action

The striving is not an extra wish attached to an otherwise inert thing. In Spinoza's account it expresses the thing's actual essence. This helps explain why preservation can include growth, learning, and changing relations: persistence need not mean freezing every present feature in place.

## Power And Its Limits

Observe the root's branching adjustment to the stone. The image suggests continued activity within constraints, not a promise that effort always prevails. Human applications need the same care: explaining desire through conatus neither makes every desire wise nor turns survival into an unrestricted moral command.

## Original literary sentence

Through the wall's defeated mortar the root continued its rootward argument, asking no permission of winter, possessing no manifesto beyond the difficult grammar of remaining and becoming itself.

## Extended reading

Imagine a musician protecting the conditions of continued practice. Rest, cooperation, rehearsal, and refusal of an exhausting schedule can all help preserve the activity through which that life expresses its capacities. The example broadens striving beyond clenched teeth or competitive victory. It also prevents a familiar error: confusing the strongest immediate impulse with the greatest enduring power of acting.

Spinoza's account belongs to a systematic metaphysics, so translating conatus directly into a modern biological drive loses part of its scope. An organism offers a vivid case, but the term does not originate as an experimentally isolated mechanism inside a nervous system. Nor is it equivalent to conscious intention. Awareness changes how human striving is understood, not whether all striving first required a reflective decision.

A useful reading exercise compares three situations: preserving a habit, abandoning a habit to retain a larger capacity, and being overcome despite sustained effort. All three complicate the slogan never give up. Conatus names an endeavor within a field of causal relations; it does not supply immunity from those relations or a simple recipe for living well.
