# 043 · derealization

The world recognized, its felt nearness altered.

## Definition

An experience in which the external world feels unreal, distant, dreamlike, or strangely altered, typically while the person retains awareness that this feeling does not establish that the world is unreal.

## Etymology

Built from de-, indicating removal, and a formation based on real; the clinical term describes an alteration in felt reality.

## Meaning In Motion

Someone experiencing derealization may recognize their kitchen while feeling that it looks staged, remote, or separated from them by glass. This describes a disturbance in felt reality, not proof of a hidden world or an automatic loss of reality testing.

## The Crucial Distinction

Derealization can occur transiently, alongside anxiety or other conditions, and in depersonalization/derealization disorder when relevant diagnostic criteria are met. It differs from depersonalization, which concerns detachment from oneself. An illustration can communicate alienation, but neither an evocative image nor an isolated description is sufficient to diagnose a person.

## Feeling Is Not A Verdict

A person can know that the room is real while finding its familiarity emotionally inaccessible. That distinction between reality testing and felt reality is central to understanding the experience. Describing the sensation as unreal should not be automatically interpreted as endorsing a delusion about the room.

## An Image Cannot Diagnose

The glasslike screen gives visual form to a possible report, not a universal symptom portrait. Experiences vary, and clinical interpretation depends on duration, distress, impairment, and context. Keep the artwork's metaphor separate from diagnostic criteria and avoid turning aesthetic estrangement into evidence of a disorder.

## Original literary sentence

The kitchen retained every cup and cupboard, yet stood a pane beyond inhabiting, familiar as a photograph of home that had forgotten how to let its owner enter.

## Reading the image

A coherent interior recedes behind a wavering foreground plane, preserving recognition while suggesting felt distance.

## Extended reading

Ordinary language makes this experience difficult to describe because real can refer both to what exists and to how present or immediate something feels. Saying the kitchen is unreal may therefore mislead a listener who hears a claim about existence when the speaker is trying to describe a change in experience. Careful explanation preserves that ambiguity long enough to resolve it.

Derealization and depersonalization can occur together, but their emphases differ. The former concerns the experienced world; the latter concerns detachment from oneself. Neither word should become a casual synonym for being distracted, imaginative, or philosophically skeptical. Their clinical uses have more specific contexts, while transient experiences do not by themselves establish a disorder.

The illustration deliberately keeps furniture coherent. It places the interpretive burden on a relation of distance rather than on spectacular distortions or frightening figures. As a reading exercise, compare the statements I know this is my kitchen and it does not feel like my kitchen. Their coexistence is not simply a logical contradiction. It reveals that recognition, ownership, emotional familiarity, and perceptual presence can come apart in experience.
