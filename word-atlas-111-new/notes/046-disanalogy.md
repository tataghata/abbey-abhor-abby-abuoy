# 046 · disanalogy

Where resemblance stops supporting the inference.

## Definition

A relevant difference between things being compared that weakens or limits an analogy, particularly when the difference concerns the very feature on which an inference from one case to another depends.

## Etymology

Formed from dis-, marking separation or opposition, and analogy, ultimately from Greek analogia, proportion or correspondence between relations among compared things.

## Meaning In Motion

Calling a computer a brain can illuminate information processing, but a disanalogy appears when an argument assumes both therefore learn or experience in precisely the same way. The important difference must bear on the proposed inference; noting that one sits in a skull and another on a desk may be irrelevant.

## The Crucial Distinction

Disanalogy does not automatically destroy every use of a comparison. It identifies where explanatory transfer stops working. Careful reasoning can retain a useful similarity while restricting its scope, separating a productive model from a misleading identification. Scope matters more than perfect resemblance.

## Relevance Before Difference

Every pair of things differs in countless ways. A disanalogy matters when a difference changes the inference being drawn from their similarity. Ask which conclusion the comparison is meant to support before listing contrasts; otherwise criticism can become a catalogue of differences that leave the argument untouched.

## Models With Boundaries

A model can remain useful after one disanalogy is identified. A scale bridge may illuminate shape while failing to reproduce every material stress. The sensible response is to restrict or revise its claims, not assume that imperfect resemblance makes all modeling worthless or all extrapolation safe.

## Original literary sentence

The model river carried their argument handsomely until someone asked it to flood, whereupon its painted banks revealed the precise and consequential distance between resemblance and the thing resembled.

## Reading the image

## Extended reading

Suppose a speaker argues that managing a country is like managing a household because both must allocate limited resources. The similarity may be illuminating at one level. It becomes less reliable when the argument transfers conclusions about currency issuance, taxation, or aggregate spending without examining differences in institutional power. The relevant disanalogy concerns the proposed inference, not whether governments live in kitchens.

This example reveals why criticism should reconstruct a comparison charitably before challenging it. A metaphor intended to explain budgeting priorities is making a weaker claim than a model offered to predict macroeconomic effects. The same difference can be irrelevant to the first purpose and decisive for the second.

A practical test has three steps. State the shared relation, write the conclusion being transferred, and identify a difference that changes whether the conclusion follows. Then consider whether the analogy survives in a narrower role. This method preserves the imaginative usefulness of comparison while preventing likeness from quietly becoming identity. In the sculpture, branching is the shared relation; living growth and manufactured joints represent different causal histories and capacities.
