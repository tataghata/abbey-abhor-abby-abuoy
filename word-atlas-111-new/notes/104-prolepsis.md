# 104 · prolepsis

The telling borrows a future before its place arrives

## Definition

An anticipation of something that normally comes later: in narrative, a presentation of a future event; in rhetoric, the advance consideration of an objection, with other specialized meanings in philosophy and grammar.

## Etymology

From Greek prolēpsis, an anticipation or taking beforehand, formed with pro-, before, and a noun related to lambanein, to take.

## Story time and telling time

A narrative prolepsis presents an event later than the story moment currently being narrated. A childhood scene may be interrupted by an account of the house's future destruction. The device concerns the order of presentation, which can move differently from the chronological sequence of events within the story.

## Prediction is not identical

A character's guess about tomorrow may remain uncertain and may never become an event. Foreshadowing can suggest a future without explicitly presenting it. Narrative prolepsis more directly takes the telling forward, although the boundaries among these devices require attention to exactly what a particular passage claims to show.

## Argument anticipates an answer

In rhetoric, prolepsis can involve raising an expected objection and answering it before an opponent speaks. This reorganizes argumentative order rather than story chronology. The anticipated objection might be fairly represented or strategically weakened, so recognizing the device should lead to an assessment of how the other position is rendered.

## Other technical lives

Epicurean discussions of prolepsis concern preconceptions, and grammatical traditions develop further specialized uses. The shared sense of taking beforehand offers orientation but not a complete definition across disciplines. A useful account identifies the kind of anticipation involved instead of treating every example as an interchangeable narrative flashforward.

## Further exploration

A narrator may interrupt a childhood scene to describe the house's eventual destruction. That is a narrative prolepsis, or flashforward, because the telling moves ahead of the current story moment. Mere suspense or a character's uncertain prediction need not function the same way. In argument, a speaker can anticipate an objection and answer it before an opponent raises it. Epicurean discussions of prolepsis concern preconceptions, a different technical use. The common thread of anticipation is helpful, but it should not erase the distinct questions each discipline is asking.

Tell a simple scene in which a child plants a tree. Then insert one sentence describing the tree fifty years later. The present action may acquire tenderness, dread, or irony without any change in what the child knows. That divergence between a character's horizon and a reader's information is one source of the device's power. Now replace the inserted sentence with “She feared the tree would die.” The passage directs attention forward, but it no longer necessarily presents a future event as part of the narrative's knowledge. Comparing the versions helps distinguish anticipation as an emotional stance from a change in the temporal order of telling.

## Original literary sentence

Years before the orchard burned, the narrator let one ash-white petal fall upon the child's unworried sleeve, borrowing tomorrow's ruin to trouble the still-unbroken grammar of today.
