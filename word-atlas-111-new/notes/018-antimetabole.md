# 018 · antimetabole

A sentence folds, and agency changes sides

## Meaning

A rhetorical pattern that repeats key words in reverse order across corresponding phrases or clauses, creating an apparent exchange of positions that can sharpen contrast, reciprocity, paradox, or persuasive symmetry.

## Word history

From Greek antimetabolē, an exchange or reversal, combining elements of opposition and change in a term inherited by rhetorical analysis.

## The reversible verbal frame

“We shape our habits; our habits shape us” repeats principal terms in reverse order. The pattern gives reciprocity a memorable form by exchanging positions and grammatical roles. Antimetabole depends on this verbal reversal, not merely on two clauses expressing opposite opinions or sharing a pleasing rhythm and balanced length.

## A crossing with repeated words

Chiasmus is commonly treated as a broader crossing of grammatical or conceptual patterns, while antimetabole repeats key words themselves. Rhetorical traditions do not always maintain the same boundary. The practical distinction remains useful when it helps specify what recurs and what changes position in a particular sentence.

## Symmetry can conceal asymmetry

The ease of reversing a sentence does not establish that the world is equally reversible. People and institutions may influence each other through very different powers and timescales. A polished antimetabole can invite insight, but its formal balance cannot substitute for evidence about causation, responsibility, or mutual dependence.

## Test each direction separately

Divide a reversible claim into its two propositions and assess them independently. Ask whether shape means the same thing in each direction and whether the agents have comparable capacities. This preserves the figure's force while checking whether its verbal symmetry illuminates a relationship or makes unequal relations look misleadingly equivalent.

## Further into the word

Antimetabole has an almost mechanical pleasure: language turns over, and the components click into a second arrangement. That pleasure can make the second claim feel inevitable. Yet the inevitability belongs to the structure of the sentence, not necessarily to the relation it describes.

A strong use of the figure often acknowledges unequal reciprocity. Habits shape people differently from how people shape habits; the contrast can deepen the thought rather than undermine it. Trouble begins when a speaker lets the repeated verb conceal those differences while drawing a conclusion that requires exact equivalence.

In close reading, attend to pronouns and grammatical roles as well as repeated nouns. Reversal may redistribute agency, possession, or dependence. The sculpture's paired forms remain recognizable while crossing to opposite stations, but their routes are not treated as proof of equal force. It is a model of rhetorical arrangement, inviting the viewer to ask what changes when an apparently stable relationship is read from its other end.

## An original sentence

We built the house around our habits, and our habits built their house around us, until the threshold could no longer remember which side had first invented shelter.
