# 085 · isomorphism

Different presentations, the same preserved structure

## Definition

A structure-preserving correspondence, typically a bijection with a structure-preserving inverse, between mathematical objects; it shows that the objects share the relevant organization even when their elements or presentations differ.

## Etymology

Built from Greek isos, equal, and morphē, form, with -ism; mathematical usage gives structural sameness a precise context-dependent meaning.

## Specify what survives

An isomorphism pairs the elements of two mathematical objects while preserving their relevant structure in both directions. The phrase “relevant structure” does real work. For graphs it may mean adjacency; for groups it includes the operation. Establish the kind of object before declaring two objects equivalent.

## Geometry can distract

Draw five vertices around a circle, then redraw them scattered across a page while keeping precisely the same connections. The pictures look different, and their edge lengths may change radically. As abstract graphs they can nevertheless be isomorphic because spatial layout was never part of the structure.

## A matching requires discipline

A proposed correspondence must be one-to-one and onto, with the required relations preserved. Matching the number of vertices and edges alone does not establish graph isomorphism. These counts are useful invariants, but distinct structures can share them, demanding a more discriminating comparison of how connections fit together.

## Equivalence has a scope

Two groups can be isomorphic without being literally the same set of elements. Calling them structurally equivalent allows reasoning to pass through the correspondence while retaining that distinction. The philosophical temptation to say “everything is the same” exceeds the precise mathematical claim being made.

## Further exploration

Two networks may be isomorphic when each vertex of one corresponds to exactly one vertex of the other and adjacency is preserved. One drawing might resemble a wheel and the other a tangled knot, yet the same connections persist under relabeling. Which features must be preserved depends on the mathematical category: groups require compatible operations, while ordered structures require compatible order. Isomorphism is therefore not visual similarity or equality of every conceivable property. It establishes sameness relative to the structure under examination, a precise rather than universal equivalence.

To test the sculpture, name its five colors and write down which colored nodes each one touches on the left. Repeat on the right. A crossing of two lines is not an additional vertex unless it has been marked as one, a convention that becomes especially important in the tangled presentation. This exercise separates perception from structure without dismissing perception: a good drawing may make the invariant easier to discover. Outside mathematics, “isomorphism” is sometimes used loosely for organizational resemblance. Such usage should say which relationships are being preserved and how completely. Otherwise a technical word for an exact correspondence becomes a flattering label attached to a suggestive analogy.

## Original literary sentence

The tangled city and the crystal garden exchanged their inhabitants house for blossom, keeping every neighborly path intact, until difference stood revealed as the costume worn by an unchanged relation.
