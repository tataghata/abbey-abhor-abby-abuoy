# 033 · catachresis

A borrowed word bends beyond its accustomed joint

## Meaning

A strained, transferred, or seemingly improper use of a word, ranging from a necessary borrowed expression where literal vocabulary is lacking to a conspicuous metaphor that crosses or unsettles familiar semantic categories.

## Word history

From Greek katakhrēsis, misuse or excessive use, related to khrēsthai, to use; rhetoric gives the apparently negative term several productive senses.

## Naming through an available analogy

Calling a chair's support a leg illustrates a traditional account of catachresis: a borrowed expression supplies a name where a straightforward literal vocabulary is lacking. Such usage can become ordinary enough that its transfer is hardly noticed. The figure therefore need not sound conspicuously strange to remain historically or analytically interesting.

## Semantic strain made audible

“The silence chewed the horizon” foregrounds a crossing of familiar categories. The words push against their ordinary combinations, inviting interpretation through a strained transfer. This use differs in effect from an established phrase such as the leg of a chair, even though rhetorical discussions may place both within catachresis's range.

## The border with metaphor shifts

Rhetorical traditions disagree over where catachresis ends and metaphor begins. Some theoretical accounts make that unstable boundary central, asking how supposedly literal language depends on earlier transfers. The word should not be reduced to a simple insult meaning bad metaphor, since necessary naming and productive invention can also be involved.

## Name the work of the strain

When a phrase sounds improper, explain which categories it crosses and what the crossing makes available. Then test whether a plain paraphrase preserves its meaning. This distinguishes expressive pressure from accidental confusion while allowing that a successful catachresis may create a relation for which no fully equivalent literal substitute exists.

## Further into the word

Catachresis raises a problem for the idea that language begins with a complete stock of proper names for everything. New experiences, technologies, and conceptual distinctions often require extending existing words. The extension may first feel awkward, then become so familiar that speakers forget its impropriety. What counts as literal can therefore have a history.

This does not make every strained phrase successful. A transfer can open a relation or merely tangle incompatible images. Evaluation asks what the phrase enables a reader to perceive and whether its difficulty earns that perception. The inherited sense of misuse becomes interesting precisely because it can identify both a failure and a necessity.
