# 103 · prosopopoeia

A made voice answers from an unavailable mouth

## Definition

A rhetorical or literary act of giving a speaking persona to someone absent or dead, or to an abstraction or nonhuman thing, thereby making an otherwise unavailable voice address the audience.

## Etymology

From Greek prosōpopoiia, making a person or persona, combining prosōpon, face or person, with a formation related to poiein, to make.

## The absent speaker arrives

Prosopopoeia gives a speaking persona to someone absent or dead, an abstraction, or a nonhuman thing. A ruined city that says “I remember” acquires an addressable voice. The rhetorical act does not merely describe the city's history; it stages a speaker who could not literally deliver that speech.

## Personification is a neighbor

Calling a winter “angry” attributes a human quality without necessarily constructing a speaking persona. Prosopopoeia commonly emphasizes assumed voice or personhood more strongly. Terminological boundaries vary, but identifying the actual device prevents a broad label from concealing the difference between a descriptive attribute and a dramatized address.

## Voice can grant attention

A poem may let an overlooked landscape or forgotten figure confront an audience. The invented speech can make a neglected perspective emotionally available. Yet this imaginative access is not recovered testimony; its persuasive power should remain distinguishable from evidence about what an actual absent person thought or said.

## Who authors the authority

The living writer chooses the words supplied to the silent or dead. That fact creates an ethical question about ventriloquism, especially when historical suffering becomes a literary resource. A work can acknowledge the constructed nature of its voice while still using the device to demand reflection or accountability.

## Further exploration

When a poem lets a ruined city say 'I remember my inhabitants,' it constructs a voice where no literal human speaker exists. Prosopopoeia overlaps with personification, but the emphasis commonly falls on an assumed persona or speech, rather than merely attributing one human quality to an object. It can also make a dead historical figure speak, raising questions about authority: who has the right to supply those words? The device can dramatize neglected perspectives while simultaneously revealing that such access is an imaginative construction, not recovered testimony.

Write five sentences spoken by a demolished building, then mark every claim whose authority depends on imagination rather than surviving evidence. The exercise need not discredit the passage. It clarifies the difference between what the building's remains document and what the author asks its persona to communicate. Next change the speaker from the building to a named historical resident. The obligations become sharper because a real person's views might be misrepresented. Prosopopoeia inhabits this productive but uneasy boundary between giving voice and taking it. The sculpture exposes its masonry instead of disguising it as a living face, keeping the constructed mouth visible while allowing the address to exert its force.

## Original literary sentence

I have housed their winters, said the broken wall, and if my stones now speak with borrowed mouths, let the living answer for the words they place among my ruins.
