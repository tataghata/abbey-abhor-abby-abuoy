# 102 · pleroma

Fullness is not simply a cupboard with nothing spare

## Definition

Fullness or plenitude; in particular ancient Christian and Gnostic writings, the term can designate divine fullness or an ordered spiritual realm, with meanings that differ across texts and theological systems.

## Etymology

From Greek plērōma, fullness or that which fills, related to plēroun, to fill, and plērēs, full; theological specialization follows contextual usage.

## Fullness before specialization

Greek plērōma can concern fullness, what fills something, or a full complement. Theological uses develop that vocabulary in particular texts. Knowing the ordinary sense helps establish a starting point, but the object of fullness and its doctrinal significance must be recovered from the context under examination.

## Distinct theological settings

Christian scriptures use the term in theological ways, while some Valentinian and other Gnostic accounts describe a divine Pleroma articulated through aeons. These uses do not amount to one doctrine shared unchanged by all Christians or by every movement later grouped under the contested category “Gnostic.”

## Completeness differs from crowding

An overcrowded room contains many things yet need not exemplify the specialized idea of divine fullness. Quantity and plenitude can come apart. The interpretive task is to ask what is complete, which relations compose its fullness, and what kind of lack or limitation the text contrasts with it.

## Images require restraint

A luminous dome can evoke order, abundance, and an interior that feels complete. It cannot establish how aeons relate in a particular system, much less reconcile different theological accounts. The image offers a mood and a relational prompt; historical explanation must supply the distinctions its visual unity leaves unstated.

## Further exploration

In ordinary Greek, plērōma can concern what fills something or its full complement. Christian scriptures give the term theological uses, while some Valentinian and other Gnostic systems describe the Pleroma as a divine realm articulated through aeons. These are related historical uses, not one doctrine shared without qualification by every Christian or every movement classified as Gnostic. Calling a crowded room a pleroma would therefore miss the specialized idea of fullness as completeness or divine abundance. The crucial question is what a particular text understands to be full, and of what.

Consider three uses of “full”: a full vessel, a full membership, and a full realization of a possibility. The word remains familiar while the criterion of completeness shifts from spatial capacity to a roster to achievement. Pleroma likewise requires asking what kind of fullness a writer means before letting the term float into an unspecified spiritual atmosphere. In a historical text, identify its contrasts and relations: does it concern divine presence, an ordered realm, or something else? Avoid reconstructing an entire system from the sound of one evocative noun.
