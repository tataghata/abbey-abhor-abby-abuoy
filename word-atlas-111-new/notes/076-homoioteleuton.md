# 076 · homoioteleuton

When matching endings make the eye forget

## Definition

Similarity of endings in neighboring words or phrases, used as a rhetorical sound pattern; in textual transmission, the term also describes omission caused by a copyist's eye skipping between similar endings.

## Etymology

From Greek homoioteleuton, having a similar ending, built from homoios, similar, and a form related to teleutē, ending or completion.

## An ending the ear expects

Neighboring phrases can acquire a family resemblance through recurring endings. “We seek clarity, stability, and accountability” uses related terminal sounds to gather distinct aims into one cadence. Homoioteleuton need not obey the meter or exact matching demanded by a particular tradition of rhyme.

## An ending the eye mistakes

A copyist looks away from an exemplar to write, then returns to find the last remembered ending. If another line ends similarly, the eye may land there instead. The intervening material disappears even though the surviving text can appear unusually smooth and plausible.

## Ornament and accident

The rhetorical figure normally involves arrangement; the textual error involves a failure of placekeeping. One makes repetition audible, the other lets repetition conceal loss. Their shared name identifies a perceptual resemblance without implying that a copying mistake secretly functions as an intentional literary technique.

## Reading the absent middle

Editors suspect such an omission when a shorter witness has corresponding terminal material around a plausible gap. Similar endings alone do not prove the history of an error. Manuscript relationships, grammar, sense, and independent witnesses must support any proposed reconstruction of the missing passage.

## Further exploration

A speaker can arrange successive phrases with matching endings so that the ear anticipates a cadence before the argument concludes. The resemblance can involve suffixes rather than the stricter pattern expected of verse rhyme. In manuscript study, the same visual resemblance becomes hazardous: a scribe reaches one ending, glances back, locates a later matching ending, and resumes after it, silently dropping the intervening passage. The rhetorical ornament and the copying error share a perceptual trigger, but they have very different intentions and consequences.

Write six short lines, ending the second and fifth with “at the gate.” Copy them onto another page, deliberately interrupting yourself after the second line. You can feel why a return to the fifth is tempting: the search is guided by a fragment that has more than one address. Such an experiment illustrates a mechanism without establishing that a particular manuscript suffered it. The ethical counterpart is editorial restraint. An attractive missing passage is still a conjecture until evidence supports it. In literature, the same resemblance that can empty a historical record can create memorable music. The word holds those opposed consequences together without mistaking shared form for shared intention.

## Original literary sentence

The copyist's candle shivered, the twin endings beckoned, and between their courteous likenesses a kingdom vanished, leaving the chronicle smooth as snow above a fallen bridge.
