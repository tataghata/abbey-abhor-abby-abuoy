# 106 · rhizome

Growth can travel sideways without one commanding trunk

## Definition

A usually horizontal plant stem that produces shoots and roots from nodes; in Deleuze and Guattari's philosophy, a model of multiplicity and connection that challenges exclusively hierarchical, tree-like accounts of organization.

## Etymology

From Greek rhizōma, a mass of roots or something rooted, related to rhiza, root; modern botany specifically identifies a modified stem.

## Botanical specificity matters

A rhizome is a modified plant stem, commonly horizontal and underground, whose nodes can produce roots and shoots. Ginger provides a familiar example. Calling it merely a root loses the distinction that makes its pattern of growth informative: new development can arise along a segmented, extending stem.

## A philosophical borrowing

Deleuze and Guattari use the rhizome to think about multiplicities, heterogeneous connections, and organizations with multiple entries. Their figure challenges the assumption that every intelligible system must derive from one trunk or a single governing hierarchy. It is an intervention in philosophical description rather than a complete botanical taxonomy.

## Networks still have power

Decentralization does not guarantee equality, freedom, or resilience in every circumstance. Networks can develop dominant hubs, exclusions, and unequal access; hierarchies can coexist with lateral connections. The concept becomes stronger when it encourages investigation of these relations instead of serving as an automatic compliment for anything that looks interconnected.

## Follow the actual pathways

Ask where movement can begin, what a break interrupts, how a new connection changes the arrangement, and who controls important passages. Such questions turn the philosophical image into an analytical prompt. They also prevent a densely branching drawing from substituting for an explanation of how a particular organization operates.

## Further exploration

Ginger's familiar underground structure is a stem, not simply a root: its nodes can generate new growth. Deleuze and Guattari borrow the rhizome to think about networks with multiple entries, heterogeneous connections, and transformations that resist a single governing trunk. Their philosophical figure is not a complete botanical description or a guarantee that every decentralized system is liberating. Real networks can contain hierarchy, constraint, and unequal power. The concept is useful when it prompts investigation of connections and breakages rather than becoming a fashionable substitute for explaining how a particular system works.

Compare a printed organizational chart with the informal ways advice travels through the same institution. The chart may have one hierarchy, while practical knowledge circulates through friendships, shared tools, and temporary collaborations. Calling those relations rhizomatic can draw attention to neglected pathways, but it does not explain their history or make them inherently benign. A well-connected informal group might help newcomers or exclude them. Trace one piece of information and record its entrances, detours, and blockages. The exercise tests the usefulness of the concept against specific movement. Likewise, the sculpture keeps recognizable nodes and shoots so that the botanical source remains present even as its lateral arrangement invites a broader philosophical reading.
