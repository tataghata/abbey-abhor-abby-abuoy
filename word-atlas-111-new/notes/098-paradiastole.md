# 098 · paradiastole

One action crosses the border between virtue and vice

## Definition

Rhetorical redescription that distinguishes closely related qualities, often recasting a morally questionable trait as an adjacent virtue or a supposed virtue as a vice, thereby shifting an audience's evaluation.

## Etymology

From Greek paradiastolē, distinction or separation, related to paradiastellein, to distinguish; rhetorical tradition applies it to discriminating or redescribing neighboring qualities.

## Redescription changes judgment

Calling a refusal “steadfastness” rather than “stubbornness” moves conduct across an evaluative boundary. Paradiastole works among neighboring moral descriptions whose distinctions are contested. It is not merely exchanging one neutral synonym for another; the changed wording proposes a different account of what deserves approval or criticism.

## The circumstances decide

Holding a position despite pressure might protect a vulnerable person or preserve a serious mistake. Facts about evidence, responsibility, consequences, and available alternatives help determine whether persistence counts as admirable. The rhetorical dispute cannot be resolved by choosing whichever adjective sounds most dignified in isolation.

## Manipulation and correction

Paradiastole can launder a vice by giving it a flattering name, but it can also challenge an unjust accusation. Distinguishing prudent caution from cowardice may be a necessary ethical correction. The device's moral status depends on the adequacy of the distinction, not simply on its persuasive effectiveness.

## Descriptions already evaluate

Accounts of conduct often combine reporting with judgment. “She abandoned the plan” and “she reconsidered the plan” can concern similar events while directing attention differently. Analysis asks what each formulation emphasizes or suppresses, then tests whether those emphases are supported by the circumstances rather than by preference alone.

## Further exploration

A politician may call stubbornness 'steadfastness,' while an opponent calls the same behavior 'inflexibility.' Paradiastole works along such disputed evaluative boundaries; it is more than exchanging arbitrary synonyms. The force depends on which circumstances justify the favorable or unfavorable description. Sometimes the redescription is manipulative, but it can also challenge an unfair judgment by making a real distinction, such as separating prudent caution from cowardice. Its ethical interest lies in the fact that describing conduct already helps judge it, so factual narration and moral assessment are not always neatly separable.

Choose a disputed action and write two descriptions without inventing new facts. One should place the action near a virtue, the other near a vice. Then identify the additional evidence that would make one description more defensible: Was contrary evidence ignored? Who bore the cost? Was there time to deliberate? This exercise demonstrates why rhetorical precision and ethical inquiry cannot always be separated. It also guards against the cynical conclusion that every moral judgment is merely verbal manipulation. Some redescriptions illuminate differences that others conceal.
