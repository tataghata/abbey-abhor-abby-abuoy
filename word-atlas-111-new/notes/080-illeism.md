# 080 · illeism

The self appears in another grammatical seat

## Definition

The practice of referring to oneself in the third person, usually by one's name or a third-person pronoun, rather than speaking in the first person; its effects depend strongly on context.

## Etymology

From Latin ille, that person or he, with the suffix -ism; the term labels a habit of third-person self-reference.

## Who is referring to whom

“Mara will take care of it,” spoken by Mara, refers to the speaker in the third person. The crucial fact is the reference relation, not the appearance of a proper name alone. Another person saying precisely the same sentence would ordinarily be reporting about Mara.

## Many motives, one construction

Illeism can sound grand, playful, ceremonial, alienated, or reassuring. A child may use a name while acquiring pronouns; an adult may adopt it for public performance. The grammar supplies evidence of a shift in perspective, but it cannot independently establish the speaker's motives or personality.

## Distance can have uses

Speaking of oneself from an observer's position may help organize reflection in some circumstances. Yet an interpretive effect is not a universal therapeutic law. Tone, audience, habit, and situation determine whether the third-person turn creates useful distance, unwanted estrangement, or nothing more consequential than a joke.

## Nearby forms differ

The royal or editorial “we” changes number while remaining first person. Third-person narration in a novel need not involve illeism at all. To diagnose the construction, identify an actual or represented speaking position, then ask whether that position names itself as a third-person referent.

## Further exploration

When a speaker says 'Mara will handle it' while being Mara, the grammatical viewpoint places the self where another person would ordinarily appear. That displacement can sound ceremonial, playful, distancing, self-promotional, or emotionally regulating; no single motive follows automatically. Historical narration and children's speech can use it without implying the same psychology. Illeism differs from the royal or editorial 'we,' which shifts number while retaining first person. It also differs from ordinary third-person fiction unless the speaker is referring to that speaker's own self.

Read “I will answer for this” and “Mara will answer for this” aloud while imagining that Mara speaks both. The second can suggest a promise made on behalf of a public identity, as though the name were an office its bearer must occupy. It can also sound evasive. The difference cannot be settled by grammar alone, which is precisely why context belongs inside the analysis. Now imagine the second sentence in an official chronicle, a playful family conversation, and a private notebook. Each setting changes the expectations of voice and authority. Illeism gives the analyst a precise starting point; it does not provide a shortcut to diagnosing the person who uses it.

## Original literary sentence

Mara, said Mara, would presently arrive, while I stood inside her name like a tenant behind drawn curtains, listening to my own footsteps being announced from the street.

## Reading the image
